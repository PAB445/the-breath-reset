Relax · Wind down

Extended exhale

In 4Out 6Time about 90 sec

Use it when
You need to come down a level. After a stressful meeting, before bed, or when your heart's still going after something that's already over.
How
Sit or lie down. Breathe in through your nose for four, then let the breath out slowly for six. If six feels like a strain, make the whole breath shorter and keep the exhale a little longer than the inhale.
Notice
When it finishes, let your breath go back to normal. Is your body heavier on the chair? Is your jaw looser? Or nothing much? Any of those answers is useful.

Relax · Find a rhythm

Coherent breathing

In 5Out 5Time about 90 sec

Use it when
Your body feels switched on and your thoughts are all over the place. The even count gives your attention something to hold.
How
Follow the circle. Five seconds in, five seconds out, through the nose if you can. Keep it light. You don't need to fill your lungs, just stay with the pace.
Notice
Take one normal breath at the end. Does it feel any different from the breath you took before you started?

Field notes

Four things worth knowing about your breath.

CO₂

CO₂ isn't just waste.

The level of CO₂ in your blood is one of the main signals your brain uses to decide when you breathe. It also affects how easily oxygen is released to your tissues, which is known as the Bohr effect. Breathe far more than you need and CO₂ drops, which is part of why over-breathing can leave you light-headed or tingly.

Pace

Slower isn't always better.

Breathing at around six breaths a minute settles a lot of people. It's one tool. Faster, fuller breathing raises activation on purpose, and learning to stay with that is a skill too.

Nose

Why the nose?

Your nose warms, humidifies and filters air before it reaches your lungs. It also adds a little resistance, which tends to slow breathing down and keep the volume closer to what your body needs.

Breath holds

What a breath hold tests.

A relaxed hold after a normal exhale gives a rough idea of how strongly you react to rising CO₂. It shifts with sleep, stress, time of day and what you've eaten. Treat it as a reference point to track, not a score or a diagnosis.
